What do we really know about Ted's students' evaluations of him? I thought that
the ones we've heard about came from an organization known to be rather harsh
towards the instructors.

I imagine that the students who were really into math would have been less
critical of Ted's teaching. There was the one student mentioned in that "Extra"
segment early this year who wrote to Ted, and told him his class changed his
life, and how much Ted appreciated getting that letter.

The most telling quote mentioned over the course of this thread is:  "He was an
unusual person, he was not like the other graduate students.  He was much more
focused about his work.  He had a drive to discover mathematical
truth." - Michigan math professor Peter Duren

I don't believe Ted's love for math ever abated. Think about how he told Danny
Wood that mathematics can explain everything in the universe. And I don't think
that a few students not liking his classes would turn him from this course. It
seems to me that since no one remembers him from high school, that he wasn't
very popular at all, and found a way to deal with it - through his math. (I am
not trying to imply that this solved everything for him - he certainly wanted
to find love - but I don't think that this had anything to do with his
students!) 

I don't see Ted as the kind of person who did things just to be popular. I see
him as someone who did what he wanted to, when what he wanted was under his
control.

I think his renunciation of his career at Berkeley was based in something much
more serious than a lack of acclaim. The more I think about it, the more his
given explanation, that he did not want to train engineers, makes me think that
he was beginning to espouse an anarchist philosophy back then. I don't know
what the anarchist movement was like at the time, but the way he describe
anarchy in paragraph 215 fits in with this thought. "The anarchist [Footnote
34] too seeks power, but he seeks it on an individual or small-group basis; he
wants individuals and small groups to be able to control the circumstances of
their own lives. He opposes technology because it makes small groups dependent
on large organizations."

Mathematics -> Engineering -> Technology!

When did he first read Conrad's "The Secret Agent"?

Ted had a lot of contradictions in his writings - but if anyone here has kept
up a journal and other writings for as long as he has, I wonder how well they
would stand up under this type of scrutiny. I put these contradictions down to
a complex personality, and a great deal of introspection.

-Susan
