                          THE VERDICT IS IN
     
Two paperback books have already been written about the UNABOM matter 
and the suspect Ted Kaczynski.  The winner by a nose in the race to the 
bookstores was Mad Genius:  The Odyssey, Pursuit, and Capture of the 
Unabomber Suspect.  It was written by the "award-winning journalists" 
and the Editorial Staff of Time Magazine.  Close behind was Unabomber:  
On the Trail Of America's Most-Wanted Serial Killer, which was written 
by former FBI profiler John Douglas and co-author Mark Olshaker.  Both 
books are fair summaries of the facts reported by the press to date.  
Mad Genius is more readable, while Unabomber has the advantage of being 
written by someone who once was involved in the investigation.   Both 
books are impressive efforts to summarize complex facts in less than two 
weeks.   Whether -- given a man's liberty at stake -- it was something 
that needed doing is another matter.  


The beginning of both books requires the reader to keep the faith and 
read on.   Mad Genius begins with an essay on the paranoiac personality.  
Although most people would agree that the Unabomber is a deeply troubled 
individual, "paranoiac" is not necessarily a word that comes to mind.   
Many view the Unabomber as both supremely rational and patently immoral.  
Similarly, Unabomber begins with a lengthy description of the original 
"Mad Bomber" case in New York City nearly a half-century ago -- it is 
clearly an attempt to demonstrate the value of profiling.  The account 
might have been more persuasive if the authors had left out the part 
where the psychiatrist gleaned from the bomber's "W's" that he lived 
with a female relative who reminded him of his mother. 
         
Neither book contains material information not previously reported.  
Unabomber represents a greater departure from previous press reports in 
that it discusses the profiling done by one of the authors.  Some 
readers (and perhaps some members of the Task Force) may be annoyed at 
author John Hunter's self-congratulatory tone.  Douglas has previously 
faulted the FBI's probe in interviews in the press.  The author argues 
that the bomber might have been caught sooner if they had just heeded 
his view (and apparently that of other profilers) that the bomber was 
more intelligent and educated than thought.   Some members of the Task 
Force, Douglas suggests, persisted in focusing on speculation that the 
bomber was a blue collar worker in the airline industry.  "Had anyone 
asked me, I wouldn't have agreed with this."  Hindsight is indeed 20/20 
  Douglas' "retrospective" psychological insights provided in the 
appendix are truly that -- gleaned with the benefit of the accumulating 
evidence against suspect Kaczynski.

Both books borrow extensively from quotes previously reported in other 
press accounts.  Neither account, however, contains the most telling  
quote.  Department of Justice spokesman Carl Stern said:  "There are 
times when we try informally to prevent something which is grossly 
untrue from being published or broadcast, but in this case  I felt I 
couldn't do even that."
      
Mad Genius has a useful list of the 700+ items found in the shed.  The 
list contains some interesting items details not previously reported -- 
some of which appear to be highly incriminating.  For example, one can 
of screws apparently contained a handwritten note that read:  "burned 
and soaked in corroding solution -- clean."  The list includes a hooded 
jacket and zippered sweatshirt that were found; one was blue and the 
other was green.   Neither book emphasizes the fact that the witness 
reported a man in a gray hooded sweatshirt -- an unfortunate lapse given 
that the headlines featured the discovery of a hooded sweathsirt and 
sunglasses as if it were dramatic evidence confirming Kaczynski's guilt. 

Unabomber contains separate sections detailing the chronology of the 
Unabom incidents and the letters written by the Unabomber.  Both 
sections are correct in their facts.    Connections are drawn to 
Kaczynski, however, only where the perceived connection is corroborative 
of his guilt.  With respect to the November 1979 and June 1980 bombings 
that were mailed from the Chicago area, no mention is made of the fact 
that, by all accounts, Kaczynski had moved to Montana in the Summer of 
1979.  And some of the alleged connections are not persuasive -- for 
example,  in connection with an April 24, 1996 bombing, the authors note 
that Kaczynski stayed at a Helena, Montana hotel on March 13.  So?

The authors of both books apparently were looking to beef up their 
length.  Mad Genius includes an essay written by computer scientist 
David Gelertner, who was severely injured by a bomb in 1993.   Unabomber 
includes a chapter titled "A Second Opinion," which quotes a behavioral 
analysis based, in part, on an examination of handwriting that is 
assumed to be the bomber's.  The note read:  "Call Nathan R -- Wed 7 
p.m."  The curious aspect of the "Second Opinion" is that the authors 
nowhere allude to whether they view the "Nathan R" note as being in 
Kaczynski's handwriting.  

Importantly, both books contain the manifesto and many people will buy 
the books just to have a copy of the manifesto. 

For those who have already been following the press on the Unabom 
matter, the  greatest strength of Douglas' book is his apt comments on 
the manifesto.   After noting the Unabomber's insistence that all modern 
technology be eliminated, Douglas writes:

"Let's be real, here, folks.  He knows that isn't going to happen.  ... 
He's got a personal ax to grind based on his own deep problems and 
inadequacies."  

As "true crime" books,  the books without a doubt prove that the man 
living in the shed was Theodore John Kaczynski.   Moreover, it appears 
clear that he had bomb-making materials in his possession.   They may 
even provide ample evidence that he was a clever and careful mailbomber.  
As for enlightening the public on the question whether the Kaczynski is 
the Unabomber, however, the books suffer the same drawback as the press 
reports.  We still do not know which of the press leaks are accurate.  
Mad Genius has this odd statement:  "From the day of the raid on, there 
was little doubt in the public mind that he and the Unabomber were one 
and the same."  This is an ironic statement for these influential 
journalists to make.  Only passing mention is made of the many grossly 
inaccurate headlines that pummelled readers in early and mid April.   
For example, just three days after Kaczynski's arrest, the  headline in 
New York Daily News of a story about the two typewriters found headline 
screamed "Typewriter bombshell."   The reports were proved wrong when 
the lab tests did not confirm the agents' initial reported impressions 
that one of the two typewriters matched the one used to type the 
manifesto.   At about the same time that the disappointing lab tests 
came back on the first two typewriters, agents found a third typewriter, 
for which the final lab tests have not been announced.  

It would have been preferable if both books had more expressly taken 
into account the possibility that some more of the unconfirmed reports 
by unnamed sources are flat out wrong.   

The Unabomber book actually repeats a number of apparently mistaken news 
items. For example, the Unabomber book repeats the story mistakenly 
first reported by the Washington Post and then nationwide concerning a 
list of names of approximately 25 University of California-Berkeley 
mathematics professors allegedly found in the cabin.  Douglas and author 
Olshaker repeated the report even though the Post, along with most other 
major newspapers, had long since forthrightly explained that the report 
was wrong.  A campus policeman had mistakenly assumed that a handwritten 
list written by an FBI agent for his own purposes had been by the mad 
bomber. Similarly, he authors repeated the apparently unconfirmed 
reports that Kaczynski's family had purchased tickets to California (as 
opposed to have given Kaczynski money).  Finally, unlike Mad Genius, the 
Unabomber authors appear to accept the widely-reported account by the 
proprietors of Royal Hotel in Sacramento that he had stayed at their 
hotel in Sacramento, even though the New York Times has persuasively 
reported that the owner has backed off the statement and had told the 
FBI that he did not know the man in the pictures he had been shown.

It is also disappointing that someone knowledgeable about 
psycholinguistic techniques would place so much stock in the use by both 
the Unabomber and Kaczynski of the phrase "You can't eat your cake and 
have it too."   Douglas describes the phrasing as "twisted" and "too odd 
and too striking to be coincidence."  The bomber's phrasing, and 
Kaczynksi's phrasing, however, is the variation used, for example, by 
James Joyce in Ulysses in the 1920's.  Although the more conventional 
phrasing of the saying is five centuries old, the version used by both 
the Unabomber and Kacyznski is hardly a mistake -- it is simply a 
variation used by many literate and educated folks.  And it is the more 
logical phrasing -- it is only remarkable if you think you can still 
have your cake after you eat it.  By definition, you need to have it 
before you can eat it.

Relatedly, Douglas' reliance on the analysis done by psycholinguistic 
types enlisted by his former colleague, Clint Van Zandt, is also 
disappointing.  Those experts relied upon only five pages of writing 
selectively provided from among hundreds written by Ted to his mother 
and brother.  We could make authors Douglas and Olshaker look like any 
number of characters --ranging from Elmer Fudd to Frank Delano Roosevelt 
-- depending on the particular five pages we chose.  Although we do not 
yet know the exact contents of the  five pages were provided,  an expert 
might have demanded all available copies before rendering an opinion.  
Certainly, an opinion that a one of several hundred million people in 
this country was 60-80 percent likely to be a serial murderer should not 
have been given without seeing all available letters.  There are several 
authors on the usnet group "alt.fan.unabomber" ("Bandit" and "Ric 
Ricardo" to name two) who sound more like the Unabomber than Ted likely 
did in the five pages provided Van Zandt's  experts). 

Such factual errors, even though minor, threaten the integrity of some 
of the conclusions reached.  For example, the  authors in Unabomber 
conclude that the note "Wu, I told you it would work . R.V." was a 
reference to two Berkeley math professors.   The conclusion (whether 
true or not)  is far less likely once one realizes that the handwritten 
list of math professors was, in fact, not found in the cabin.  Mad 
Genius is not without its curious statements and conclusions.  For 
example, the authors in Mad Genius summarily conclude that the same note 
attached to a 1982 bomb was "probably inadvertently" left by the bomber.  
The more natural inference would be that it was left as a red herring, 
and was intended to mislead the Task Force. 

Both books make the same error that press reports have been making since 
April of last year -- the bomber, according to the conventional wisdom, 
was jealous of the attention paid to the Oklahoma City bombings.  The 
Oklahoma City bombing was April 19.  The Unabomber sent a sophisticated 
bomb on April 20 along with some fairly lengthy letters.  One of those 
letters announced that he had a lengthy manifesto (and he gave the 
approximate word count) that he would be forwarding.  It is unlikely 
that the bomber woke up on April 19 and after hearing of the explosion 
in Oklahoma said: "Boy, I'm glad I brought this typewriter to California 
with me on the bus so I could send out these letters and type these 
labels." 
        
Mad Genius shows a greater willingness to state the facts in ways that 
depart from the previous accounts.  For example, the journalist-authors 
boldly (and probably correctly) report that 22 people were injured 
rather than the 23 usually recited in the UNABOM folklore.  It was 
probably the psychology professor in 1985 that they didn't count.  He 
was merely standing by as his teaching assistant who opened the package, 
and was not, in fact, injured.  More significantly, Mad Genius casts a 
healthy dose of skepticism on a report by a man in Salt Lake City that 
he stayed at the same hotel as Kaczynski in 1978.  The fellow, now a 
karate instructor, is still waiting to keep the pompous bomber in a dark 
alley.  And to the author's credit, they raise a collective eyebrow at 
the report by the owner of the Sacramento hotel who, they note, "was 
less sure of himself when talking to investigators." 

Unabomber, however, is not without its advantages.  Not only are any 
errors of minor significance in the overall story, but the chronology 
and letters section (both of which bear an uncanny resemblance to the 
sections on a certain webpage),  serve as very useful guides in 
understanding the facts of the matter.  Moreover, Unabomber but not Mad 
Genius  includes the recent description by Professor Saari concerning 
his recollection that he had met Kaczynski, and that Kaczynski had vowed 
to get even after being treated rudely by engineering professors to whom 
Saari had referred him.  Finally, Unabomber, can easily lay claim to the 
best line: "Throughout the 1967 Summer of Love, Ted Kaczynski didn't 
have a single date."

Unabomber ends with a plea in favor of the significance of the type of 
psychological profiling done by the principal author.  One question, 
however, arises that has never been addressed by the authors or the 
press:  each week the public views the homicides presented in shows like  
NYPD Blue and Homicide.  Was the $50 million plus spent on this case 
worth it?  He hadn't made an attempt on an airline in nearly 15 years.  
It is not uncommon for several people to  die  on a given night in 
certain parts of our major urban centers.  Why, again, should we care 
about this particular murderer?

Any provable mistakes -- and there are factual inaccuracies in both 
books -- are truly minor given the wealth of detail that was summarized 
in such a short period.   On the most important issue of whether the 
manuscript was found, we can only wait to see if the leaks prove true.   

The conflicting accounts provided by these two books of the alleged 
discovery  demonstrate that such confidence may rest on a shaky 
foundation.  Mad Genius describes it as a carbon copy while Unabomber 
describes it as a "master" copy from which the Post and Time copies were 
typed.   A third view -- offered by a number of California papers -- is 
that the document appears to be an early draft -- and that it is neither 
a carbon copy or a master draft.  Four other copies were mailed, and so 
it seems unlikely to have been a carbon copy.  And the draft sent to the 
Post and NY Times  apparently was identical even in its typographical 
mistakes, and so it seems unlikely to have been a "master" copy from 
which the others were typed.  The intriguing question, then, if the 
books and the press reports are wrong on this  point, might they be 
wrong altogether?  Might the draft have merely been a writing with an 
anti-technological theme?  Might the report in both books that a carbon 
copy of the letter to the New York Times was found also be mistaken?  
Finally, might the dramatic report of a handwritten "hit list" -- 
airline industry", "computer industry" and "genetecists" -- recounted 
only in Mad Genius be mistaken?  None of these critical pieces of 
evidence were detailed on the inventory of items taken from the cabin, 
even though it is quite detailed in other respects.

We will have to wait and see whether the evidence leaked by "sources 
close to the investigation"  bears up to scrutiny.  In the meantime, 
those interested in the UNABOM matter should go out and buy one or other 
of the books, and then be sure not to serve on the jury in any 
prosecution.
