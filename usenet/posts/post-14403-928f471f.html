Alan Gore wrote:
"I can see this technology being used for, for example, to cause a sheep to
grow implanted human clone organs for use in human transplants. This is evil
because...?"
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
-------
Well, for one thing, there are concerns about causing cross host diseases,
which could result in epidemics. For example, say there is a microbe whose host
is a sheep, and which may or may not be a serious threat to the sheep. In the
natural world, the microbe may not have any contact with humans. But, under the
conditions of cloning which mixes human genes with cellular material from other
species, the microbe could adopt a new host, namely human beings. While the
microbe may not have been a problem for the sheep, it could be a deadly killer
for humans. It may be no life saver at all, but the cause of a plague.

People in Europe are trying to oppose the import of genetically engineered food
(available at your local domestic supermarket, in case you didn't know it) for
similar reasons (crop destruction due to unknown variables being highest). (See
Business Week, Feb. 2, 1998, in the Science and Technology section, pages
62-63.)

Quite honestly, more thought is put into testing new software than is put into
what we do to our environment, which,  if changed too much, will no longer
provide life support for this society.

To paraphrase TK (on the principles of history), if a change is made that is
large enough to alter permanently a long-term trend, you cannot predict the
consequences of such changes. I think this applies here. The natural system of
genetics is surely a long term trend, as old as the earth itself.

I'm not even going to get into the ethical issues. But many pro-technology
people will admit that you have to draw the line somewhere.
