Role in capture haunts Kaczynski's brother 

     By Cynthia Hubert
     Bee Staff Writer 
     (Published Jan. 19, 1997) 

       One is a "cool-headed logician," a brilliant but unemotional
scientist who seems never
     to have known true intimacy.

     The other is a social worker, a sensitive counselor of troubled
youths who has steeped
     himself in the lives of others.

     Today, Theodore and David Kaczynski, who grew up together but
remain strangers in
     many ways, live in their unique versions of hell.

     Theodore, 54, who bonded with nature in ways he never did with
people, is locked in
     a stark jail cell in Sacramento, accused of being the calculating
and murderous
     Unabomber.

     David, 47, lives with the horrifying reality that he helped put his
older brother behind
     bars, and that a conviction could lead to his only sibling's
execution.

     "I deeply love Ted, and that love has been made more poignant, more
real in some
     ways by the tragedy of this whole situation," David Kaczynski told
The Bee last week.

     In one of his first lengthy interviews since his brother's arrest
in April, David Kaczynski
     talked about his complex relationship with his brother, his
conflicted feelings about his
     role in the Unabomber case and his vision of justice. He spoke
softly, between long
     pauses, clearly uncomfortable with the glare of publicity that has
been cast upon him.

     At times, he smiled warmly as he recounted happy times with his
brother, whose genius
     IQ led him to Harvard at 16 and a brief stint as a mathematics
professor at Berkeley
     before he retreated to the life of a hermit.

     They played friendly but competitive games of Monopoly and chess,
David recalled,
     and performed duets, with Ted on the trombone and his little
brother on the trumpet.
     They loved word games, and Ted punned incessantly.

     While other young people listened to rock 'n' roll, Ted preferred
classical music by
     Vivaldi and Bach that "had mathematical perfection and symmetry,"
his brother said. "I
     can't ever recall him singing songs or listening to lyrics."

     The brothers grew up in a middle-class, Midwestern home led by
Wanda and Ted
     Kaczynski, strong Democrats and "talkers" who spoke often of the
value of education
     and of the need to do what is right. "They weren't rigid
disciplinarians and by and large
     I don't think they needed to be," said David. "Neither of their
children ever created
     problems in the community or problems in school." Both parents were
warm and
     nurturing, he said.

     As a young boy and adolescent, Ted was often "moody" and would
retreat to his attic
     bedroom for long stretches, rebuffing attempts by his parents to
find out what was
     wrong, David said. 

     Yet Ted was capable of small acts of kindness, his brother said.
One of his earliest
     memories of Ted was when David was a toddler and his brother
fashioned a special
     knob on the family's screen door that the younger boy could reach.

     One summer day when Ted was home from college, the brothers took a
long walk with
     their father in the woods near their home in Iowa, picked wild
blackberries and
     gooseberries and later excitedly baked pies.

     "We said later that those were the best pies we ever tasted," said
David.

     But those tender moments were rare, he said, and he described Ted
as isolated,
     withdrawn and "terribly disturbed" for most of his life. He rarely
"acted out" or showed
     violent tendencies as a child, his brother said. But he seemed to
become angrier and
     more alienated as he grew older. For most of the past three
decades, he lived as a
     recluse in Montana.

     "I have always had an uneasy feeling that for all his development
as a mind, as a
     thinker, something was wrong with Ted's emotional development,"
said David,
     speaking in the Washington, D.C., office of his lawyer Anthony
Bisceglie. "He has
     always had a lack of connection to others, an inability to
understand others." 

     He said his brother never had an intimate friend, and retreated
deeper into his shell
     when family members expressed concern about his emotional state or
his isolation.
     Nearly 10 years ago, Ted wrote his brother a venomous letter
stating, in capital letters,
     that he never wanted to see or hear from David "or any other member
of our family"
     again. He was angry because his brother was getting married.

     Ted did not attend the funeral of his father when he committed
suicide five years later,
     though he "expressed condolences" to his mother over the telephone,
David said.

     "Ted is an extraordinarily complex person," he said. "His mind is
like a building with
     many rooms, many doors. It's as if all the parts of a full human
being are there, but they
     are not integrated into a whole personality."

     Theodore John Kaczynski has pleaded not guilty to charges that he
is responsible for
     bombings that killed computer store operator Hugh Scrutton in 1985
and timber
     lobbyist Gilbert Murray in 1995, both in Sacramento, and seriously
injured academics
     in San Francisco and in Connecticut in 1993. Trial is scheduled for
next November in
     Sacramento. He also is charged in a separate case with killing New
Jersey advertising
     executive Thomas Mosser with a package bomb that was mailed to his
home. 

     Devices placed or mailed by the Unabomber injured 21 other people
between 1978
     and 1995, but Ted Kaczynski has not been charged with those crimes.

     Since his arrest in April, Ted has refused any contact with his
brother or their mother,
     Wanda, 79, David said. He wondered aloud how the man who lived as a
hermit for 30
     years in the wilds of Montana is coping with his new environment at
the Sacramento
     County Jail.

     "Ted has always hated noise," he said. "Noise is something that is
terribly, terribly
     disturbing to him. I know how tormented Ted could be by noise in a
normal human
     environment, and in jail all of that is intensified. So I'm afraid
it's much worse for him." 

     In the woods, Ted hunted and gathered much of his food, rode a
bicycle for
     transportation, and had no electricity or indoor plumbing. He kept
records of every
     penny he spent and once calculated his cost of living at about 30
cents per day, his
     brother said. 

     "He was very proud of that," he said.

     "I think that Ted sensed in the wilderness a connection with
something good, something
     larger, that many of us get from our interactions with other
people," David said. "It was
     something benign and free and good, and to be cut off from that
resource for him
     would be extremely painful."

     Nine months after government agents arrested Kaczynski at his
ramshackle cabin near
     Lincoln, Mont., ending one of the costliest and most intriguing
manhunts in history, his
     brother said he has some regrets about his role in the case. But he
has not wavered in
     his conviction that he "did the right thing" in reporting his
strong suspicions of Ted to
     authorities.

     "I regret what I did every moment, every time I think about it," he
said. "I regret Ted's
     suffering. On the other hand, I don't think there is anything I
could have done
     differently."

     He went to authorities because "I could not have lived with myself"
if another person
     were hurt and killed by a bomb that he might have prevented.

     David's inner torment, he said, has been made worse by the belief
that he and his wife,
     Linda Patrik, may have unwittingly helped fund some of the bombings
with loans to
     Ted. And the invasive media coverage of the once private family has
"made it all even
     more surreal."

     "It has been very, very painful," David said, acknowledging that he
has had bouts of
     depression in recent months. "At times I wondered how I could
function."

     But with the support of his family and friends, he said, "I have
bounced back."

     An assistant director of a program for homeless children in
Schenectady, N.Y.,
     Kaczynski took some time off this fall to process his feelings
about what had happened,
     he said.

     "I needed some space that was purely mine," he said. He also wanted
to take some
     time to assist lawyers in the case, to respond to an avalanche of
letters that he had
     received from supporters, and to write letters to family members of
the Unabomber's
     victims.

     "I thought about what the victims may have suffered, and it sunk
into me. It felt so sad
     and so tragic that I wanted to reach out to them in some way. I
wanted to express
     sorrow for what had happened."

     David Kaczynski, while stopping short of saying that he is
convinced that his brother is
     the Unabomber, discussed his suspicions and the evidence that he
took to authorities.

     He recalled a discussion that he had with Ted in 1969, when the two
went to Canada
     to look for land to purchase. "He talked a good deal about his
sense that our culture,
     basically through technology, had taken a wrong direction," he
said. "That was the first
     time that I had any sense that he had given any thought to a social
or cultural agenda."

     A year later, he wrote a 23-page paper calling for an end to
scientific progress, and
     proposed that he and David launch "an organization dedicated to
stopping federal aid"
     to such research.

     The paper, which is certain to be a centerpiece of the case against
Ted Kaczynski, said
     that people's interactions "in relation to technology" are a threat
to human freedom.

     David recognized a strikingly similar theme 25 years later in the
Unabomber's widely
     published anarchist manifesto. The document criticized the
industrial revolution as "a
     disaster for the human race" and said scientific progress had to be
stopped at all costs. 

     The author of the densely worded treatise expressed disdain for
loud noises and said
     society must revert back to "wild nature." 

     The ideas were startlingly familiar, David thought with increasing
horror. "I saw a lot of
     the same desperation that I sensed in some of the letters that he
had written to us," he
     said.

     But it was not only the theme, but some of the specific words and
turns of phrase that
     led him to believe that his brother might be the author, he said.
"It just sounded like his
     words, it sounded like his rigid, methodical intellectualizing of
things."

     Three words, in particular, caught his eye: cool-headed logicians.

     "But it is obvious that modern leftist philosophers are not simply
cool-headed logicians
     systematically analyzing the foundations of knowledge," reads an
excerpt from the
     35,000-word manifesto's introduction. "They are deeply involved
emotionally in their
     attack on truth and reality." 

     A chill went through David when he read that paragraph. He
remembered how Ted
     had chided him in the past for inserting emotions into decisions.
"You are no
     cool-headed logician," he had told him. 

     "I thought, 'Who else have I ever heard use that phrase but Ted?'
No one."

     "It's got to be him."

     But he "went back and forth" with his suspicions, in part because
he did not feel Ted
     capable of inflicting violence on another person.

     "We are not talking about someone who took pleasure in hurting
people," said David.
     "He certainly is not a person who could have taken a knife and
slashed someone. That
     would have been impossible for Ted."

     Finally, after reading the document several more times, he was
convinced enough to
     pursue the matter further. He didn't tell his mother about his
suspicions, even though she
     had previously told him that "the thought has crossed my mind" that
Ted might be the
     bomber.

     "My reasoning was that I was still hopeful that Ted could be ruled
out," David said. "I
     didn't want her to worry and I knew it would be agonizing for her
if I approached her. I
     didn't want her to be involved in the decision. How awful to be
responsible for your
     brother being arrested, spending his life in prison or, God forbid,
executed? How much
     more terrible for a mother to be in that position."

     He consulted a handwriting expert, who concluded that his
suspicions were well
     founded. Only then did he approach Bisceglie, a friend of a family
friend, who advised
     him to contact authorities.

     Some members of the task force investigating the serial bomber were
doubtful that
     Kaczynski was a credible suspect, his brother said. Authorities
agreed to monitor him
     clandestinely, which they did for weeks before apprehending him in
his hand-built
     shack. Among the items they discovered inside: a completed bomb.

     In the days leading up to and following his brother's arrest, David
said, he was anxious
     and sorrowful. 

     "I recall dreaming about it. I recall having lots of dreams about
Ted," he said. "I recall
     waking up many mornings with that awful feeling of doom and saying
to myself, 'It's not
     a bad dream. It's real. It's real.' "

     Seeing television images of the disheveled hermit being led out of
his cabin was a
     shock, he said. "The images of his tattered clothes, of his
appearance, gave me this
     awful sense of woe that this person I do love and have been close
to has really
     deteriorated. He looked so isolated, so disconnected. I felt
terrible, just terrible."

     Now it is David Kaczynski's goal to do everything he can to prevent
his brother from
     being put to death if he is convicted, he said. 

     Bisceglie said the family will address a federal team reviewing the
case and will argue
     that it would "not be fair or just" to execute Theodore Kaczynski.

     First, David Kaczynski's pivotal role in the case should be
strongly considered,
     Bisceglie said. 

     Second, the family will argue that Ted is mentally unstable, a
"mitigating factor" in
     federal death penalty cases, Bisceglie said.

     Third, the government would discourage others from coming forward
with information
     about crimes committed by loved ones by demonstrating that doing so
would result in
     the execution of their loved one, he argued.

     "I don't think that anyone wants to believe that justice is a cold,
ruthless engine," David
     Kaczynski said. "I did what I did because there was hope that there
would be some
     consideration of my brother's mental fragility and emotional
disturbances."

     David, for his part, said he would "suffer in the extreme" if his
brother were given the
     death penalty.

     "I would be plunged into hell for the rest of my life," he said,
"and I don't think I
     deserve that."
