An example of intermediate technology can be found for the farmer. The
market for cheaper, organically grown, food is clearly present. People
are concerned with the pesticides mentioned before, and organically
grown foods usually taste better than foods heavily doused in
chemicals. If a farmer organically grows wheat on his farm, and has a
local market to sell it in, why not generate a small-scale milling
technology that will allow him to easily make flour from his wheat? He
could then be fairly self-sufficient in terms of his own production of
a useful product desired by the population. A shift toward individual,
small-scale, technology would not impede decentralization, and would
be safer for the environment.

Hand-in-hand with a shift in technological goals, Schumacher proposes
a shift in society. How can society function in a manner that shifts
concern for nature and human happiness into the forefront? Schumacher
argues that a more decentralized society will be more self-reliant,
and will therefore be more flexible in achieving such a goal. Minimal
structures, he argues, lead to minimal administration. Schumacher does
not propose a helter-skelter destruction of all large-scale technology
or the capitalist system. Rather, he argues that we must decide what
our goals are in life, and what we want our goals to be. If we decide
in favor of humanism, we must examine our organizations, and decide
which elements can be effectively separated into another organization.
We need to figure out exactly what each organization's goal is, and to
decide upon an appropriate technology that will help us work toward
that goal. Finally, Schumacher argues that a company that is
democratically run by all the workers will be superior to the current
system of management. When people have real input into their
workplace, he argues, they will enjoy their work more. If they enjoy
their work, they will become more productive, and be more inclined to
work for the good of the people and of the company.

An alternative anti-technology view argues for change along similar
lines to Schumacher's. The agrarian idealists argue that technology
ruins our sense of virtue, and destroys our sense of nature. However,
unlike Schumacher, some of them want almost to completely eradicate
all forms of high-technology. They argue that a complete conversion to
an agrarian society is the only way to gain virtue and peace. Indeed,
ever since the United States was created, people have argued along
similar lines. One of the most famous of these proponents was Thomas
Jefferson, who wrote that ``Dependence begets subservience and
venality, suffocates the germ of virtue, and prepares fit tools for
the designs of ambition.'' By this, Jefferson was arguing that any
sort of dependency on factory technology was a concern; it would cause
man to disregard any virtue, happiness, and reverence for nature.
Jefferson wrote that such men would become seduced away from moral
goals by industrialization, seduced away from realizing the full
humanistic and virtuous potential of man:

  He acquires a fondness for European luxury and dissipation and a
  contempt for the simplicity of his own country; he is fascinated
  with the privileges of the European aristocrats, and sees with
  abhorrence the lovely equality which the poor enjoy with the rich in
  his own country: he contracts a partiality for aristocracy or
  monarchy; he forms foreign friendships which will never be useful to
  him, and loses the season of life for forming in his own country
  those friendships which of all others are the most faithful and
  permanent.  (quoted in [p. 131]Marx:1964)

The arguments appeal to our moral sense and to our appreciation of
nature. It is important to note that the pastoral ideal is not the
same as primitivism. The pastoral ideal recognizes the benefits gained
by small scale technology. Instead of pursuing productivity through
technology, the pastoral ideal urges pursuit of virtue through working
on the land. Technology is merely an aid to man, not a replacement for
him. Production is not the goal of man, the pursuit of virtue and
happiness is.

Norgaard argues that many people have looked at the new technological
advances, and at the corresponding social changes, and become
despondent over affairs. They are ready, Norgaard asserts, to find a
new way of life in the technological era. This idea fits well into the
Unabomer's argument that society needs to reform its attitude toward
technology in order for people to regain their identity. Norgaard
warns us that the collapse of the Soviet Union, while it was pursuing
modernity, is not a consequence isolated to socialistic economies. The
difference between the Soviet Union and the United States in this
aspect, he warns, is not very great. There is only the difference of
how quickly socialist or capitalist systems will break down into chaos
[Norgaard 1994, p. 184-185]. In order to survive, Norgaard thinks that
we must reexamine the entire relationship between our modern social
system and nature. These relationships deeply affect how we think
about the world, and about our place in the world. Instead of
dominating and controlling nature, Norgaard urges us to learn to
coexist in harmony. We must, he concludes, learn how to care for the
world [Norgaard 1994, p. 189].

Davis also argues that we must reexamine our position in nature. Along
lines similar to Norgaard, he argues that we do not need to destroy
all technology, but we must reexamine our place in the world and how
our technology affects it:

  We must, therefore, abandon our arrogance and learn from the past,
  from traditional civilizations which were self-sufficient and did
  not glorify consumption and the transformation of nature as
  ends-in-themselves. This does not mean, it should be pointed out, a
  total rejection of technology or a return to the cave [Davis 1981,
  p. 234].

Throughout his book, Davis argues that society takes a nihilistic
approach toward the development of technology. This attitude, he
argues, causes problems within society, and leads to widespread
destruction of the ecology. Davis thinks that, in order for society to
survive, people must set limits on technology by reshaping their
values and ideals, i.e., people must become humanistic instead of
nihilistic. Davis calls this new ideal Technological Humanism, and
writes that it must encompass a broad scope of values. It must, he
argues, urge man to respect his fellow man and respect nature, and
really examine how any new technology we develop might affect society
or nature [Davis 1981, p. 230-233]. Over-arching all of these
arguments is the belief that people in a society need to rethink their
priorities. People need to concern themselves with the well-being of
the society first, instead of concerning themselves with increasing
their personal wealth, perhaps at the expense of others. An increased
awareness of what people want in life, of what it means to be human,
is a goal infinitely more worthwhile than the philosophy of
materialism.

The republicanism of the early 1800s conceived that industrial growth
without the betterment of society was dangerous. The means and rates
of production were never more important than the concern that mankind
needed to improve itself mentally, morally, and spiritually. Invention
and discovery, were a means to an end, not an end in and of itself.
The real core set of problems with technology stems from our attitude
toward it. Modern man views technology as an improvement to society in
an absolute sense, and asks no questions about its possible
consequences. We are no longer viewing technology as an aid toward
improving ourselves, but are calling any technological advances [Smith
1996, p. 3].

Schumacher's ideal of small self-sufficient communities which take
special care of the ecology, Norgaard's idea to reshape how we view
our place in nature, and Davis's argument that we must abandon our
arrogance toward nature are all examples of clear goals that change
our attitude toward technology. These authors do not simply point out
the problems caused by industrialism, saying we must get rid of the
physical technology. Instead, they point toward some of the underlying
causes of why technology has become such a twisted influence upon
society. Those underlying problems, those attitudes, are what need to
be attacked; otherwise man will always have a problem determining his
place within nature and society. Without the goal of improving
ourselves, of caring for nature, and of reexamining our beliefs about
technology, we will not be able to solve any of the real problems the
Unabomer is concerned with.


The Question of Terrorism

Finally, we must discuss whether or not we can justify the Unabomer's
physical attacks on people. We have shown that the Unabomer's
anti-technology philosophy is not unique, and in fact has a much more
refined set of arguments upon which he could have drawn for support.
However, the only reason the Unabomer's manuscript generated so much
interest was because he was using bombs. Can the bombings be
considered a legitimate form of getting the attention of society, of
attacking the system and a legitimate part of the Unabomer's
revolution?

First of all, we must consider what we want to call legitimate
terrorism. Terrorism usually takes place in the form of coercion
against what is perceived as an unjust government. The government,
which practices what may be called the law of the stronger [Kropotkin
1978, p. 90], is firmly entrenched in society, and is willing and able
to destroy any visible challenge to its power. The only reasonable
solution is a sort of guerilla warfare against the state. I would
argue that, in its most basic form, the legitimacy of the terrorist
must stem from the fact that there is no rational expectation that
change can occur through institutionalized legal systems. In other
words, there is no other recourse available to the revolutionary but a
physical attack against the system.

In order for terrorists to convince a group of their legitimacy, there
can, and probably must, also be a moral element to their actions. G.
Tarnovski, i.e., G. Romanenko, an advocate of terrorism who belonged
to the Narodnaya Volya group that killed the Czar in 1881 [Laqueur
1978, p. 48], argues that terrorists must be determined that a minimum
of suffering should occur during a revolution. He writes that in a
popular revolution, the soldiers and innocent die while the
``perpetrators of evil,'' those whose actions incited the revolution,
stand by and watch the blood flow [Tarnovski 1978, p. 80-81]. But
terrorist revolution does not run along similar lines. Terrorism, he
argues, strikes against the perpetrators of evil directly. When this
strike occurs, and the people who suffered are relieved of their
burden, the reason and benefits for the revolution become clear to
them. The result is popular support for the revolution [Tarnovski
1978, p. 81-82].

Following along these lines, any form of legitimate terrorism must
have some form of political and sociological goal, otherwise the
brutal acts are simply murder. Furthermore, by definition, the
terrorist does not have the power to force the society to accept his
views. We have to conclude that this goal must be accepted by the
public before the terrorist revolution can succeed. So, the terrorist
has two problems. Once he has an ideological goal and target, he must
both destroy the target and gain popular support for performing the
act [Maitron 1978, p. 96]. Without the latter, the system is unlikely
ever to change. If there is no possibility of gaining popular support,
the terrorist will only be killing people toward no useful end.

Clearly we immediately run into a problem where the United States is
concerned. The United States government does not physically suppress
criticism of its political system. In fact, our very constitution
guarantees our right to criticize the government, and our right to
criticize the society as a whole. We can see that there is already
some popular support against technology in our society. One only needs
to examine the popularity of authors like Aldo Leopold and E. F.
Schumacher to discover it. At least some sections of the society are
obviously open to criticism of technology, and want to change our
society. These are not radical examples, but my point is that they do
not need to be radical. All that the terrorist such as the Unabomer
needs to gain legitimacy for his actions is a group of people who
agree with his means and end goals. If the Unabomer already has
support for his end goals - the restructuring of society and
technology - we only need to examine whether or not his means can also
be supported by the groups within the population.

The Unabomer considers himself to be a revolutionary. His manuscript
is an attempt to justify the revolution. It is an attempt to convince
people that the revolution is necessary and just. In summary, we know
what the Unabomer is revolting against, and we have summarized his
attempt to win public support (in Chapter 3). This leaves us with the
question of legitimacy. Can his illegal actions, his choice of victims
be justified?

Perhaps it would help if we examined some other definitions of
terrorism. Two separate attempts at defining terrorism are below. I
think they offer an interesting insight into this problem of
legitimacy:

  Terrorism is political, goal-oriented action, involving the use or
  threat of extraordinary violence, performed for psychological rather
  than material effect, and the victims of which are symbolic rather
  than instrumental ...  ... Terrorism involves the intentional use of
  violence or the threat of violence by the precipitator(s) against an
  instrumental target in order to communicate to a primary target a
  threat of future violence. The object is to use intense fear or
  anxiety to coerce the primary target into behavior or to mold its
  attitude in connection with a power (political) outcome [Chamers
  1978, p. 268].

It is clear that neither of these new definitions has any pretense of
being specifically against a repressive government. Terrorism simply
becomes violence as a means of coercion, regardless of the target. One
should also notice that these definitions do not attempt to claim the
political legitimacy that our previous definition maintained. Instead
of being a means to fight against a repressive enemy, terrorism is now
simply a violent tool to use against any other force. These new
definitions resolve the problem of whether or not they have legitimate
targets by simply dropping the question of moral rights altogether: a
target is now whomever the terrorist thinks will be effective toward
coercing the population into giving him what he wants.

I think these two definitions of terrorism are fairly useful in the
context of the Unabomer, but we also need to resolve the conflicts
between the two definitions. The first definition clearly states that
the targets are symbolic, as opposed to instrumental. The second
definition says the exact opposite. I think that it is easiest to say
that both definitions might be equally valid, but that we can select
only one for the Unabomer. In his manuscript, the Unabomer writes that
it would have been impossible for his anti-technology message to get
delivered without some form of violence: ``In order to get our message
before the public with some chance of making a lasting impression,
we've had to kill people'' [FC 1995, para. 96]. We can conclude that
his targets were really more symbolic than instrumental, the people he
attacked were considered little more than useful tools to get the
public to pay attention to his demands. Therefore, his terrorist
actions fit the former definition: a goal-oriented action against
symbolic targets for psychological effects.

Another important fact is that we cannot simply say that the Unabomer
is working against an unjust and repressive government which blocks
reform. The Unabomer does write about reform as opposed to revolution,
and argues that revolution is the easier way to change society: ``A
reform movement merely offers to solve a particular social problem A
revolutionary movement offers to solve all problems at one stroke and
create a whole new world; it provides the kind of ideal for which
people will take great risks and make great sacrifices'' [FC 1995,
para. 141]. Finally, the Unabomer writes that he is fighting the
technological system, not the political system: ``This is not to be a
POLITICAL revolution. Its object will be to overthrow not governments
but the economic and technological basis of the present society'' [FC
1995, para. 4]. The reader should ask himself how we can have a
revolution against the economic and technological fiber of our
society, without necessarily disrupting the governmental structure of
a representative democracy. There will always be people who want
technology. How can their wishes, and their political expression of
those wishes, coincide with the anti-technology movement? The Unabomer
does not give us an answer to this question.

As stated above, the Unabomer claims that he is not attacking the
government, but the commonly accepted conception of technology's place
in society. He is working to destroy modern technological society. The
Unabomer is not attacking any specific injustice, perpetuated by any
particular person, but is fighting against what he considers to be a
range of injustices; and he considers technology to be at the root of
the problem. His terrorist attacks were a simple, but effective, means
of getting public attention, and his manuscript is an attempt to win
popular support. He only makes the vaguest of pretense of legitimizing
his choice of victims, and we can conclude that they are merely
symbolic of the technological society he wants to destroy.

I can easily say that the Unabomer's tactics were successful in terms
of getting his manifesto published. But can we consider his tactics to
be legitimate? Some terrorists argue that the means justify the ends
[Fleming 1982, p. 24]. The logic is that the attacks are simply the
tools for moving toward a better world.

We can now conclude that, in order for the Unabomer's attacks to be
legitimate, he needed to have a goal, and to have symbolic targets for
psychological effects. But here we run into a problem. Were the
bombings meant to get the manuscript published, or were they meant to
help fuel an anti-technology revolution?

If it were the latter, I can only decide that his actions were not
justifiable. I have seen very little evidence that any sizable part of
the population has become concerned with technology because of the
Unabomer's essay. However, if Unabomer's only goal was to get his
manuscript published and read by the general public, then he succeeded
to an amazing degree. His writings would probably never have been
published if it were not for the bombings, and if somebody had
published his manuscript, it is unlikely that it would have been given
any attention by society. In attacking his targets, and in eluding the
FBI, the Unabomer was able to force some of the public to read his
manuscript.

Obviously, Unabomer believed that publication of the essay was all
that needed to be done in order to get public support. After the
manuscript was published, there was a brief flurry of interest, and
then it was promptly forgotten by many people. Leopold and Schumacher,
on the other hand, used clear and well founded arguments and appeals
to win support from a significant portion of U.S. society. Their works
generated discussion and new ideas, essentially they generated public
support through discourse, not violence. Their beliefs, at least in
part, became part of our society. The Unabomer has not been able to
duplicate their accomplishments.

Violence cannot attack the real problems with technology in society.
The opposition is not the scientists or industrialists in society,
they are merely products of society's attitude toward technology.
Modern technological society believes that technology is a vital
function of our general well-being [Smith 1996, p. 15], and trying to
kill all the scientists is not going to change that. What needs to
happen is a public discourse about the merits of technology and of
improving ourselves. Davis, Leopold, Marx, Norgaard, and Schumacher
all understand this. The Unabomer does not. In order to change
society, we cannot coerce it, we must convince it.


Conclusion

The Unabomer has managed to evoke strong responses from many people.
Many condemn him, some understand his anti-technological sentiment but
disagree with his violent methods, and others actually admire and
respect him. Some admirers even formed an organization called UNAPACK,
and tried to win support for the Unabomer as a write-in for the 1996
United States Presidential elections:

  The Unapack campaign is no joke, and we will go all the way with it
  to make an impact. The goal of the campaign, as with Clinton and
  Dole, is to win the election. We started actively campaigning at the
  New Hampshire primary, [see the New York Post article ``Lost
  Cause''] followed by the Massachusetts one, and we had a presence of
  about 15 signholders at the Weld/Kerry debates April 8th, relating
  to the senatorial campaign with signs that said, ``How about a real
  debate?''... [Eccles 1996]

However, in the end, I think the Unabomer failed to find support in
the general public. The reader of the manifesto finds that the
Unabomer fails to go much beyond urging anyone who harbors an
anti-technology resentment to reproduce, urging him to fight against
high technology, and warning him not to associate with leftists.
Beyond this, he fails to properly draw out the form that the
revolution could take, and fails to properly address concerns about
how technology can be removed from society.

Unabomer could have drawn upon powerful visions of the future written
by such luminaries as Schumacher, and used the convincing arguments of
men like Norgaard to buttress his anti-technology argument. I think
that, while Unabomer managed to point out some serious flaws with
technological society, he really failed to get at the root of the
problem. Unabomer states that the revolution does not have to be
violent, or political. He argues that the revolution would only change
the view society takes toward technology. But what does this new view
consist of? While he points out very real problems - e.g., alienation
and depression of the worker - and while he raises very real concerns
about society, I do not think that he presents a convincing proposal
showing how his revolution would be an effective solution to those
issues. He failed to really examine why society emphasizes technology
and production as much as it does. He may claim to attack our
world-view, but without explaining the problem, the attack is not
going to register with society. Unabomer's revolution does not offer a
clear-cut solution to the societal problems.

With a solid outline of what steps could be taken in order to form a
society free of high technology, with a real vision of the future and
of new ideals, Unabomer could probably convince people that his ideas
hold real possibilities. With a viable plan of attack, and a firm goal
outlined, I think Unabomer stands a better chance of winning his
anti-technology converts. Until then, I think society will happily
write off his manifesto as the delusions of a madman, and they will
ignore the core set of problems that the Unabomer is concerned with.
The source of society's discontent, the source of many of these
problems may be traceable to technology, but if one examines the
problem further, one sees the real problem is in our conception of
what is important in life. When I started this thesis, I had stated to
people that ``His way of thinking is so alien to me.'' I did not
understand how somebody could be so anti-technology. What I discovered
was that his concerns over society, the worker's malaise, the
destruction of the environment, the instability of society, are all
very real concerns. But I do not think that the solution is to attack
technology. The solution is to attack how we think about technology.

One should realize that, while the Unabomer's arguments may not be
convincing, men like Schumacher, Marx, Davis, and Norgaard raise some
real concerns and propose feasible solutions to those concerns.
Instead of using an external force, such as a physical revolution, to
tear down technology, they argue that we could try to redirect
technological development through a purely mental revolution. We could
start to examine what place technology has in our own lives, and try
to relate that to the benefits or harm it could present to society or
nature. Our goal, therefore, is to think long and hard about what we
use technology for, and what we want to use it for.

American society placed technology upon a pedestal called progress,
and we began to believe that progress was the most important virtue of
society. Instead of becoming a practical means toward political or
moral ends, technology became its own, ever growing, force of change
in society [Marx 1996, p. 253]. The republican virtues have been
subsumed by the belief that technology is an improvement to society in
and of itself. Unless we change our attitude toward technology, and
regulate it back to being a tool, not a driving force, we will be
unable to solve the real problems that the anti-technology advocates
are concerned with. More people in society need to question
technological materialism, and temper their optimistic beliefs in
modernity with a concern about how to define man's place in the world.
We need to carefully examine the role of technology in society before
we can hope to understand and change any of the problems technology
causes society and nature. Before we can do any of this, we need to
change our attitude toward technology.

Timeline

  May 25-26, 1978:
  A package is found in a parking lot at the University of Illinois in
  Chicago and is taken to Northwestern University in Evanston because
  of the return address. It explodes when it is opened on May 26,
  injuring Terry Marker, a security guard.

  May 9, 1979:
  Graduate student John Harris is injured by a bomb at Northwestern
  University.

  Nov. 15, 1979:
  Twelve people suffer smoke inhalation when a bomb explodes in a
  727's cargo hold during an American Airlines flight, forcing an
  emergency landing at Dulles International Airport near Washington.

  June 10, 1980:
  United Airlines President Percy Wood is injured at home in the
  Chicago area.

  Oct. 8, 1981:
  A bomb is found in a business classroom at the University of Utah in
  Salt Lake City. No one is injured.

  May 5, 1982:
  Janet Smith, a secretary, is injured by a bomb at Vanderbilt
  University in Nashville. The package was addressed to computer
  science professor Patrick Fischer.

  July 2, 1982:
  Diogenes Angelakos, a professor of electrical engineering and
  computer science, is injured in a faculty lounge at the University
  of California at Berkeley.

  May 15, 1985:
  Graduate student John Hauser is injured by a bomb in computer room
  at the University of California at Berkeley.

  June 13, 1985:
  A package-bomb that was mailed to the Boeing Co. in Auburn, Wash.,
  on May 8 is discovered and disarmed.

  Nov. 15, 1985:
  A package-bomb mailed to University of Michigan psychology professor
  James McConnell injures his assistant, Nicklaus Suino. McConnell,
  standing nearby in his Ann Arbor home, is not hurt.

  Dec. 11, 1985:
  Hugh Scrutton, 38, is killed by a bomb near his computer rental
  store in Sacramento.

  Feb. 20, 1987:
  Gary Wright is injured by a bomb left behind a computer store in
  Salt Lake City.

  June 22, 1993:
  Charles Epstein, a geneticist at the University of California at San
  Francisco, is injured by bomb sent to his home.

  June 24, 1993:
  Computer scientist David Gelernter of Yale University in New Haven,
  Conn., is injured in an office.

  Dec. 10, 1994:
  Advertising executive Thomas Mosser, 50, is killed by a bomb sent to
  his North Caldwell, N.J., home.

  April 24, 1995:
  California Forestry Association President Gilbert Murray, 47, is
  killed opening a mail bomb in the group's Sacramento headquarters.

  June 24, 1995:
  Letter mailed to the Washington Post with the manuscript. Letter
  mailed to the New York Times with the manuscript. Letter mailed to
  Penthouse publisher Bob Guccione.

  June 24, 1995:
  Letter mailed to the San Francisco Chronicle - airliner threat.

  Last week of June, 1995:
  Letter mailed to the magazine, Scientific American.

  June 28, 1995:
  Letter mailed to the New York Times. Letter mailed to the Washington
  Post.

  June 29, 1995:
  Letter mailed to Tom Tyler, a University of California, Berkeley,
  social psychology professor.

  April 3, 1996:
  Theodore John Kaczynski, 53, taken into custody by federal agents.

  April 5, 1996:
  Kaczynski charged with possessing the bomb components and held
  without bail.

  April 30, 1996:
  Kaczynski appeals to Supreme Court to be released due to government
  leaks.

  June 18, 1996:
  Kaczynski indicted in Unabomer attacks.
