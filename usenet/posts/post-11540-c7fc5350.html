  I'm in more than 90% agreement with you.  Among many of my interests
  are those addressing appropriate technology use yet I still find many
  "open questions" pro/con the Unabomber debate.  I begin by observing
  that the accompanying terrorist issues are moot since publication of
  the Manifesto was in context to the Unabomber's promise -not- to
  continue terrorist acts.  So the Manifesto must instead be read as a
  pacifist document and evaluated/asessed independently of terrorism.

  The Manifesto isn't poorly written -- and I think critics too readily
  turn toward counter-argument -- yet it has a fundamental 'flaw' in its
  failure to provide an accurate definition of technology.  Perhaps an
  "accurate definition" is however itself a technological act and all
  we have to go on is what he has already provided, so we must trundle
  along simply on its literary merits.  Stanford's Professor McCarthy
  has made an astute observation that the Unabomber's victims were not
  really sterling examples of modern 'technologists' responsible for
  his perceived ills so that feature (among many quiddities surrounding
  the Unabomber/Kaczynski saga) casts into doubt whether the Unabomber
  had a thoroughly precise notion of implications to his own diatribe.

  I'm also in agreement that an array of choices and selection pressures
  are inevitable in any kind of society so that the particular variety
  and -degree- of variation are not necessarily moral issues.  I do not,
  however, agree that the market forces are necessarily our best tools
  for determining adaptability or survivability of appropriate genetic
  constituents, as the artificial 'market' is not in correspondence to
  a natural order with the ecological environment.  Technologists have
  gained an 'upper hand' so they do not stand in much need of defense
  while those of cultural diversity among (non-technological) arts and
  humanities, as 'underdogs' in a technological society, are those most
  appropriately defended against transgressions by the strong. Microsoft
  has sustained recent anti-trust lawsuits on account of competing web
  browsers while the -real- anti-trust lawsuits consist of those yet to
  be presented by 'non-technological' disciplines against the technology
  elitists.  Furthermore, as Professor McCarthy notes, future opportunity
  with high-technology computer development is dwindling so that fewer
  need to be involved at ground-zero designing or building technology
  applications for the rest of us.  An increasing proportion of jobs
  with high-technology will be those in operations and support rather
  than computer programming and hardware development, due to present
  software in proliferation and also to CPU speeds already attained.

  Even if one objects to Kaczynski's terrorist tactics one cannot deny
  the much larger numbers of individuals who are being killed one way
  or another as technology spin-offs.  I don't speak merely of auto
  accidents, or global pollution, or decreasing economies of scale to
  overpopulation, or the likelihood of nuclear armageddon in context to
  the prospect of global overpopulation, or the savaging of planetary
  natural resources, or the decimation of artisan guilds following the
  industrial revolution, or the 'factory mentality' wrought upon those
  workers exploited by capitalist masters, or the destruction of family
  values by television, or a fundamental separation from 'nature' that
  wants to convert wilderness into theme parks.  I speak also of those
  trained to work in high-technology fields who were chewed up and spit
  out by the system, as lemons squeezed dry and turnips having yielded
  up blood, thrown back onto the street without so much as a thank you.
  That's the fate of those who believe high-technology will save them.
