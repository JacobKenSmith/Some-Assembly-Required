	On Jacques Ellul, he is a very interesting, but also frustrating
author.  He can completely contradict himself withing a single
sentence.  However, when he writes on something, he shows himself to
have thoroughly mastered the literature that has gone before, and always
has a unique view of his own.  He seems to have been a chronic rebel. 
Briefly a Communist, he became a devotee of the Reformed Church of
France while still a student.  Under Vichy rule, he criticized the
Nazis, and was fired from his academic job.  Joining the underground, he
became one of the movement's most prominent figures, and is credited
with smuggling 200 Jews out of the country.
	After the war, he did not join fellow Resistance leaders in positions
of national prominence, but criticized some of them (especially Sartre)
for engaging in fashionable, profitable forms of social rebellion.  It
took him four years to find a publisher for his classic attack, "The
Technological Society" which was (and is) often assigned reading in
courses about the subject.  It is still in print.
He also wrote "The Technological System," expanding on the earlier book,
and "The Political Illusion," criticizing leftist utopians.  Popular
with the American New Left, he rebelled against his own popularity by
insisting (in "The Ethics of Freedom") that technology was merely the
modern form of evil and sin, and that only salvation through Christ
could free the individual.  A couple of clear paraphrases of Ellul by
FC:

	Ellul --- "In a given civilization, technical progress is
irreversible." (Tech. Soc. p.89; originally in italics)
	FC --- "But technological advances are permanent withing the context of
a given civilization." (Ind. Soc. ���133)

	Ellul --- "The technical phenomenon cannot be broken down in such a way
as to retain the good and reject the bad." (Tech Soc, p.111)
	FC --- "The 'Bad' Parts of Technology Cannot Be Separated From the
'Good' Parts" (Ind. Soc. section title for ���121-4)

	Perhaps more importantly, FC appears to be using the word "technology"
the way Ellul did, which is to mean "all forms of technique."  That is
why FC can include forms of psychology and bureaucracy as part of the
system.  They are "techniques."
	Without going on forever, I will mention two other points of interest. 
First, FC may have started this whole project as a rejection of Ellul's
Christian perspective.  "The Ethics of Freedom" is basically a book on
theology, but it often digresses to criticize a position that is rather
similar to the one that FC later endorses.  For instance, Ellul insists
that power and freedom cannot be reconciled with one another, FC defines
freedom as the opportunity to go through the power process.  Ellul
connects anarchists to his earlier criticism of leftists by calling them
utopian.  FC is at pains to show that their anarchism is not utopian.
	Second, "The Technological Society" was translated into English at the
suggestion of Aldous Huxley, and there is a special note in the
bibliography citing the influence of fiction writers, including Huxley
and Orwell.  The "Translator's Introduction" points out that the logical
opposite of Ellul is B.F. Skinner (author of "Walden Two" and "Beyond
Freedom and Dignity").  One of FC's targets was an avid Skinnerian
psychologist, and "Industrial Society" defends "freedom and dignity" and
"freedom and autonomy."  Ellul was a long-standing analyst and critic of
science fiction (he despised 2001, Fahrenheit 451, and Alphaville
because they were too optomistic for him).  Note that the phrase "Brave
New World" is in "Industrial Society" and capitalized just as is the
phrase "True Believer" which is also the title of a book.  Ellul treated
all sorts of technical control, with long sections on economics and the
state.  FC's treatment of control is narrower, I suspect, because FC has
focussed on forms of control that appear in "1984," "Brave New World,"
and "Walden Two."
	On another topic.  I think FC's mention of Nazis is just a case of
including them as symptoms of social breakdown.  The left is seen as a
special threat, not because they are the primary enemy, but because they
are revolutionary competitors.
	As for my suggestion that an SDS veteran might be involved with FC, the
point is that SDS was considered part of the New Left, but it was built
around the ideas of C. Wright Mill, and was run as a decentralized,
small-group, participatory democracy, which means it was semi-anarchist
in practice.  It was destroyed by a battle over whether to purge a
Maoist group that had systematically infiltrated SDS.  Anyone strongly
on the pro-purge side would have an ax to grind against leftists, but
would be wise to cite foreign examples of leftist subversion rather than
one that might be self-identifying.
